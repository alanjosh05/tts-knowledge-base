1What is text-to-speech?

In 1939, at the New York World's Fair, an operator sat at a strange keyboard and made a machine called the Voder say sentences by pressing keys and a foot pedal. Operators trained for months to play it, and it sounded like a ghost. Eighty years later, a phone can read a book aloud in a voice you'd mistake for a person. This guide is the story of how we got from one to the other, and how the modern version works inside.

Text-to-speech (TTS), also called speech synthesis, is any system that takes written text and produces spoken audio. You meet it every day:

  • Accessibility: screen readers for blind and low-vision users; reading aids for dyslexia.
  • Assistants: voice assistants, car navigation, smart speakers, phone menus.
  • Media: audiobooks, video narration, podcasts, dubbing, game characters.
  • Devices: robots, kiosks, toys, home-automation alerts, often running offline.
  • Conversation: the voice half of AI chat companions and voice agents.

Four generations of talking machines

  1. Rule-based (1960s–1990s). Hand-written rules describe how the mouth and throat shape sound (formant synthesis). Clear but robotic. Famous example: the voice Stephen Hawking used.
  2. Concatenative (1990s–2010s). Record a person reading for many hours, chop it into tiny pieces, and glue the right pieces together (unit selection). Natural in places, with audible seams elsewhere, and huge databases.
  3. Statistical parametric (2000s–2010s). A statistical model (first HMMs, later neural networks) predicts a compact description of the sound, and a vocoder turns it into audio. Smooth and small but muffled.
  4. Neural (2016–today). Deep networks learn the whole mapping from data. WaveNet (2016) showed neural networks could produce audio that sounds human; Tacotron (2017) learned to go from characters to sound directly. Every modern system descends from these.

Today's TTS is neural, and that's what this guide teaches.

2The journey of a sentence

A sentence wants to be heard: “Hello! I'm Junco, a tiny voice that runs entirely on your own device.” On the page it's 70 characters. To reach your ears it must become air pushed back and forth thousands of times a second. No single step can make that leap well, so the sentence travels through a small factory with five workshops. Each does one job and hands a new kind of object to the next.

Click each workshop, or press Auto tour. Everything is real: the phonemes come from espeak-ng and the pictures are computed from the actual audio of a small neural voice.

The five workshops in plain words

  1. Text. What you type. Letters are a poor guide to sound: “read” (now) and “read” (yesterday); “Dr.”; “$5”.
  2. Text front end. Cleans the text (normalisation: “$5” → “five dollars”) and converts words to phonemes, symbols for sounds. Chapter 5.
  3. Acoustic model. The neural network at the heart of TTS. It decides how everything is said (duration, pitch, loudness) and outputs a picture of sound called a mel spectrogram. Chapters 9–11.
  4. Vocoder. A second network that “plays” the picture, producing the waveform. Chapter 12.
  5. Speaker. The waveform moves a speaker cone, which moves air, which reaches you.
The analogy to remember. The mel spectrogram is sheet music; the waveform is the performance. The acoustic model is the composer who writes sheet music for this exact sentence; the vocoder is the musician who plays it.

Why draw a picture first?

One second of this clip is 24,000 waveform numbers, but only about 94 spectrogram columns. Predicting 94 smooth, slowly changing columns is far easier than 24,000 numbers that swing up and down hundreds of times per second. So the creative work happens in the small picture world, and a specialist vocoder does the fussy last step. This split is the most important design decision in classic neural TTS; only a few “end-to-end” models (chapter 13) remove it.

3Sound as numbers

Clap your hands. For a split second, the air near your hands is squeezed, then stretched, then squeezed again, and that ripple of pressure races across the room at 343 metres per second. Your eardrum is pushed in and out by it. A microphone does the same thing as your eardrum, but writes down how far it was pushed, thousands of times a second. That list of numbers is digital audio.

Waveform

A waveform is that list: each number (a sample) is the air pressure at one instant, usually scaled between −1 and +1. Plot them and you get the familiar wiggly line.

Sample rate

The sample rate is how many samples are taken per second, in hertz (Hz). 24 kHz means 24,000 samples per second. Common speech rates are 16 kHz (speech recognition), 22.05 kHz and 24 kHz (TTS), and 44.1 or 48 kHz (music, studio).

Frequency and pitch

A sound that repeats 200 times a second has a frequency of 200 Hz; we hear higher frequency as higher pitch. A typical adult voice has a basic pitch (the fundamental frequency, F0) of roughly 85–255 Hz, but its timbre, what makes “ah” differ from “ee”, lives in higher frequencies called harmonics and formants, up to several thousand Hz.

Try it: sampling a wave

Orange dots are samples. The dashed line joins them. Drop the sample rate below twice the wave frequency and the dots trace a different, slower wave: that's aliasing.

The Nyquist rule

To capture a frequency, you need more than two samples per cycle. So a sample rate can only represent frequencies up to half of itself, the Nyquist frequency. 24 kHz audio holds sounds up to 12 kHz, which covers almost all of what matters in speech. That's why TTS rarely needs 44.1 kHz: double the numbers, little audible gain for voice.

Bit depth and loudness

Each sample is stored with some precision: 16-bit audio has 65,536 possible levels. Loudness is measured in decibels (dB), a logarithmic scale: every +6 dB is roughly double the amplitude. Our ears work logarithmically too, which is why TTS models work with the log of energies (next chapter).

Go deeper: the numbers in code 
import soundfile as sf
wav, sr = sf.read("clip.wav")      # wav: numpy array of floats in [-1, 1]
print(sr)                          # e.g. 24000 samples per second
print(len(wav) / sr)               # duration in seconds
print(wav[:5])                     # the first five air-pressure values

Resampling (e.g. 22,050 → 24,000 Hz) is done with a band-limited filter such as torchaudio.functional.resample; never by dropping or repeating samples, which causes aliasing.

Check yourself

Audio is sampled at 16 kHz. What is the highest frequency it can hold?

Nyquist: half the sample rate, 8 kHz. That's why 16 kHz speech sounds a little dull but is fine for recognition.

4Pictures of sound: spectrograms and the mel scale

A piano chord is several notes at once. If you only saw the waveform, you'd see one tangled wiggle. A pianist's sheet music instead shows which notes are playing when. A spectrogram is sheet music for any sound: time runs left to right, pitch runs bottom to top, and brightness shows how loud each pitch is at each moment.

From waveform to spectrogram

  1. Cut the waveform into short overlapping slices called frames, e.g. 1,024 samples long (~43 ms at 24 kHz), starting a new one every 256 samples (the hop, ~10.7 ms).
  2. For each frame, ask “how much of each frequency is in here?” using the Fourier transform. The whole procedure is the short-time Fourier transform (STFT).
  3. Stack the answers side by side. Each column is one frame; each row is one frequency.

With a hop of 256 at 24 kHz you get 24,000 / 256 ≈ 93.75 frames per second: the “94 columns” from chapter 2.

The mel scale: bending the picture to fit our ears

We hear the step from 100 Hz to 200 Hz as huge, but 8,000 to 8,100 Hz as nearly nothing. The mel scale stretches low frequencies and squashes high ones so equal steps sound equal. A mel spectrogram groups the STFT's frequencies into 80–128 mel bins (100 here) using triangular mel filters, then takes the logarithm so loudness, too, matches hearing.

Try it: Hz versus mel

The curve is mel = 2595 · log₁₀(1 + Hz / 700). The tick marks are 20 mel bins spaced evenly in mel: crowded at low Hz, sparse at high Hz, just like our hearing.

Reading a real mel spectrogram

  • Horizontal stripes stacked evenly are the voice's harmonics; they rise and fall with the pitch (intonation).
  • Bright smudges high up with no stripes are noisy sounds like “s”, “sh”, “f”.
  • Dark vertical gaps are silences: pauses, or the closure before “p”, “t”, “k”.

Look back at the explorer in chapter 2, station 3, and find each of these.

Go deeper: why the mel is “lossy”, and the phase problem

The STFT gives two things per frequency: magnitude (how loud) and phase (where in its cycle the wave is). The mel spectrogram keeps only magnitude, and blurs frequencies into bins. You can't simply invert it; something must invent plausible phase and fine detail. The classic algorithm is Griffin–Lim (iterative, metallic-sounding); modern systems use a neural vocoder (chapter 12).

import torch, torchaudio
mel_fn = torchaudio.transforms.MelSpectrogram(
    sample_rate=24000, n_fft=1024, hop_length=256, n_mels=100, power=1.0)
mel = mel_fn(torch.from_numpy(wav).float()[None])   # [1, 100, frames]
log_mel = torch.log(mel.clamp(min=1e-5))            # what TTS models predict

Important: your acoustic model and vocoder must agree exactly on sample rate, n_fft, hop, number of mels, magnitude vs power, and log base. A mismatch produces garbled audio. Easiest rule: compute training mels with your vocoder's own feature extractor.

5From letters to phonemes: the text front end

Imagine reading aloud a note that says “Dr. Lee lives on St. James St. and owes $1,250.50 by 5/6.” You quietly expand it in your head: Doctor Lee lives on Saint James Street and owes one thousand two hundred fifty dollars and fifty cents by May sixth. Then, for each word, you know which sounds to make. A TTS system must do both steps explicitly, before any neural network sees the text.

Step 1: normalisation

Text normalisation rewrites everything that isn't plain words: numbers, dates, times, currency, units, abbreviations, URLs. It's mostly rules (plus some ML for ambiguous cases like “St.” = Saint/Street). Errors here are the most common cause of embarrassing TTS mistakes.

Step 2: grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P)

English spelling is unreliable: “though”, “through”, “tough”, “cough”. A phoneme is one unit of sound; writing words as phonemes removes spelling's confusion. A pronunciation dictionary (like CMUdict) covers common words; a G2P model or rule system such as espeak-ng handles the rest. The output usually uses the IPA alphabet:

Step 3: symbols to numbers

Networks only take numbers, so every symbol (phoneme, stress mark, punctuation, space) gets an ID from a fixed list, the vocabulary. “həlˈoʊ” becomes something like [14, 6, 22, 3, 29, 31]. Punctuation is kept because it tells the model where to pause and how to intonate (“?” rises).

Characters or phonemes?

Some models read raw characters and learn pronunciation themselves. That needs much more data, and they still mispronounce rare words. For small models and small datasets, phonemes are the reliable choice.

6Neural networks in one story

Picture a huge mixing desk with millions of tiny knobs. Sound goes in one end, comes out the other, and each knob nudges the signal a little. At first the knobs are random and the output is noise. You play a sentence in, compare the output with the real recording, and a clever helper tells you, for every knob, “turn this slightly left, that slightly right, and the output gets a bit closer.” Repeat this a million times. That's training a neural network. The knobs are called parameters or weights.

The neuron and the layer

A neuron multiplies each input by a weight, adds them up, adds a bias, and passes the result through a simple bend called an activation function (ReLU, GELU, …). Without the bend, stacking layers would collapse into one straight-line calculation; with it, deep networks can model almost anything. A layer is many neurons side by side; a linear layer is the weights-and-bias part written as a matrix multiply: y = W·x + b.

A tensor is just a multi-dimensional array of numbers. In TTS you'll see shapes like [batch, time, channels]: several sentences, each a sequence of steps, each step a vector of numbers.

The rest of Part B introduces the specific layers TTS models are built from.

7The layers, one by one

Every TTS architecture, however fancy, is assembled from a small toolbox. Learn these eight and you can read any paper's architecture diagram.

Embedding: a word becomes a point in space

Each phoneme ID looks up its own row in a big table of learned vectors (say 192 numbers). Similar sounds end up with similar vectors, the way cities that are close on a map have similar coordinates.

Used for: turning phoneme IDs, speaker IDs, language IDs or emotion tags into vectors. nn.Embedding(n_symbols, 192)

Convolution: looking at the neighbours

A 1-D convolution slides a small window (e.g. 5 steps wide) along the sequence and computes the same weighted sum at every position. Like reading with a magnifying glass that shows a few letters at a time.

Used for: local context: how a sound changes next to its neighbours (co-articulation); fast decoders and vocoders. Cheap and parallel. Stacking convolutions, or dilating them (gaps inside the window), widens what they see.

Recurrent layers (LSTM, GRU): reading with a memory

Reads the sequence one step at a time, carrying a memory forward, like you reading a sentence word by word.

Used for: older models such as Tacotron 2. Mostly replaced by attention and convolutions because they can't process steps in parallel, so training and inference are slow.

Self-attention: everyone looks at everyone

At a dinner party, each guest (position) asks a question (query), every guest holds a name card (key) and something to say (value). Each guest listens most to those whose name card matches the question. Every position can gather information from any other position, in one step.

Formula: Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(Q·Kᵀ / √d) · V. Multi-head attention runs several of these in parallel so different heads can track different things (rhythm, syntax, emphasis).

Used for: text encoders that understand the whole sentence (a question mark at the end changes the first word's intonation), and large decoders.

Cross-attention: the text-to-sound bridge

Same idea, but the questions come from one sequence (sound frames) and the name cards from another (phonemes): “which phoneme should I be saying right now?”

Used for: learning alignment in Tacotron-style models (chapter 9) and conditioning in modern transformers.

Positional information: where am I?

Attention on its own is a bag of guests with no seat numbers. We give each position a seat number, either added to the input (sinusoidal or learned positions) or built into attention (rotary embeddings, RoPE).

Normalisation and residual connections: keeping deep stacks healthy

A residual connection adds a layer's input back onto its output (x + f(x)), so each layer only has to learn a small correction, and signals and gradients flow through dozens of layers. Normalisation (LayerNorm, RMSNorm) rescales activations to a steady range, like an automatic volume control between layers.

Used for: every modern deep network. A transformer block is: norm → attention → add residual → norm → feed-forward MLP → add residual.

Upsampling and the length regulator: from words to frames

Text has ~15 phonemes per second; mel has ~94 frames per second. Somewhere the sequence must get longer. The length regulator simply repeats each phoneme's vector for as many frames as that phoneme lasts: if “h” lasts 4 frames, copy it 4 times. Vocoders do the same jump again (94 → 24,000 per second) with transposed convolutions or an inverse STFT.

Go deeper: one transformer block in PyTorch 
import torch.nn as nn
block = nn.TransformerEncoderLayer(
    d_model=192, nhead=2, dim_feedforward=768,
    dropout=0.1, batch_first=True, norm_first=True)   # pre-norm, the stable modern default
# x: [batch, time, 192]; padding_mask: [batch, time], True where padded
y = block(x, src_key_padding_mask=padding_mask)

8How a model learns

You're blindfolded on a hilly field and want to reach the lowest valley. You can't see, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. Take a small step downhill. Again. Again. That's gradient descent. The height of the ground is the loss: a single number saying how wrong the model currently is.

  • Dataset: pairs of (text, recording). For one voice, 10–25 hours of clean, consistent single-speaker audio is a classic amount (LJ Speech has about 24 hours); fine-tuning an existing model can work with far less.
  • Loss: for a spectrogram, often the L1 loss (average absolute difference between predicted and real mel) or L2/MSE (average squared difference).
  • Backpropagation computes, for every weight, which way to turn it (the gradient).
  • Optimiser: Adam/AdamW takes the step, adapting step size per weight. The learning rate sets how big steps are; warm-up starts small, gradient clipping stops sudden giant steps.
  • Batch: several utterances processed together. Sentences differ in length, so shorter ones are padded, and a mask tells every layer and the loss to ignore the padding.
  • Step / epoch: one update / one pass over the whole dataset. Small TTS models typically train for tens to hundreds of thousands of steps.
  • Validation: hold out some utterances the model never trains on. If training loss keeps dropping while validation loss rises, the model is overfitting (memorising).
  • EMA: keep a slowly updated average copy of the weights; it often sounds smoother than the latest weights.
  • Mixed precision: doing most maths in 16-bit floats to train faster on GPUs.
Golden rule: the loss going down does not guarantee the voice sounds better. Always listen to samples during training, and measure with the tools in chapter 15.

Check yourself

Training loss falls but validation loss rises. What is happening?

It is memorising the training set instead of learning patterns that generalise.

9Acoustic models: two families

Two artists are asked to paint a speech's sheet music. The first paints one column at a time, always looking at what it just painted, deciding as it goes when to move on to the next sound. The second first plans exactly how many columns each sound gets, then paints every column at once. The first is autoregressive; the second is non-autoregressive (parallel).

Autoregressive: Tacotron 2 (2017)

Tacotron 2 [paper] made neural TTS sound close to human. Its parts:

  • Encoder: character embeddings → 3 convolution layers → a bidirectional LSTM.
  • Attention: location-sensitive cross-attention decides which characters to “look at” for the next frame.
  • Decoder: an LSTM that predicts one mel frame at a time, fed its previous frame through a small pre-net. A post-net of convolutions polishes the result, and a stop token predicts when to end.
  • Vocoder: a modified WaveNet.

Strengths: natural prosody; no need to know durations in advance. Weaknesses: slow (one frame after another), and attention sometimes fails: skipping words, repeating them, or never stopping, especially on long or unusual sentences.

Non-autoregressive: FastSpeech 2 (2020)

FastSpeech 2 [paper] removes the step-by-step loop:

  • Encoder: phoneme embeddings → transformer blocks.
  • Variance adaptor: small predictors for each phoneme's duration, pitch and energy. The length regulator expands phonemes to frames using the durations.
  • Decoder: transformer blocks produce all mel frames in parallel.

Strengths: very fast, robust (it can't skip or repeat, since every phoneme gets its frames), controllable (change speed or pitch by editing predictions). Weakness: it needs to know the true duration of every phoneme in the training data. Where do those come from? That's alignment, the next chapter.

Check yourself

Why can't FastSpeech 2 skip or repeat words like Tacotron sometimes does?

Durations are predicted per phoneme and expanded directly, so nothing depends on a fragile attention path.

10Alignment: which sound happens when?

You have a recording of “cat” lasting 30 frames and three phonemes, k-æ-t. Which frames belong to “k”? Nobody labelled them. Finding that out is alignment, and it's the quiet problem at the heart of every TTS model.

Three ways to get alignment

  1. Learn it with attention (Tacotron): flexible, but can fail.
  2. Use an external aligner like the Montreal Forced Aligner, a speech-recognition-style tool that outputs timestamps per phoneme (FastSpeech 2's approach). Accurate, but an extra pipeline step.
  3. Search for it during training: Monotonic Alignment Search (MAS), introduced by Glow-TTS [paper]. No external tool, no fragile attention.

How MAS works, in plain words

The model predicts, for each phoneme, what an “average” mel frame for it looks like. Then for every (phoneme, frame) pair it scores how well that frame matches that phoneme. MAS finds the single best path through this score table that obeys speech's rules: start at the first phoneme, end at the last, move forward only, never skip a phoneme. That's a classic dynamic programming problem, the same family as finding the shortest route on a map. The path's length on each phoneme is that phoneme's duration, which then trains a duration predictor for use at inference.

Try it: Monotonic Alignment Search

Rows are phonemes, columns are mel frames. Brighter cells = the frame matches that phoneme better. Step 1 fills a running “best total score” left to right; step 2 walks back from the last cell, always to the better of “same phoneme” or “previous phoneme”. Raise the noise and watch MAS still recover sensible durations.

Go deeper: MAS in 20 lines 
def mas(log_p):                      # log_p: [T_text, T_mel] numpy array
    T, M = log_p.shape
    Q = np.full((T, M), -np.inf); Q[0, 0] = log_p[0, 0]
    for j in range(1, M):            # every frame
        for i in range(min(j + 1, T)):   # phonemes reachable by frame j
            stay = Q[i, j - 1]
            move = Q[i - 1, j - 1] if i > 0 else -np.inf
            Q[i, j] = max(stay, move) + log_p[i, j]
    path = np.zeros((T, M)); i = T - 1
    for j in range(M - 1, -1, -1):   # walk back
        path[i, j] = 1
        if j > 0 and i > 0 and (i == j or Q[i - 1, j - 1] >= Q[i, j - 1]):
            i -= 1
    return path                       # path.sum(1) = frames per phoneme

The score is a Gaussian log-likelihood, log_p[i, j] = −½‖mel_j − μ_i‖², where μ_i is the phoneme's predicted average frame. In real training code this loop is compiled with Numba or Cython for speed.

Check yourself

Which rule does MAS enforce on the alignment path?

Monotonic (forward only) and complete (no skips): exactly how speech unfolds.

11Why voices sound flat, and generative decoders

Ask a thousand people to say “really?” and you'll hear a thousand melodies: surprised, bored, sarcastic. Now train a model with L1 loss to predict “the” melody. The safest guess, the one with the lowest average error, is the average of all of them: a flat, blurry compromise nobody actually says. That's over-smoothing, and it's why simple models sound muffled and monotone.

The fix is to stop predicting one answer and instead learn to sample from the whole range of good answers. Models that do this are generative models. Five families appear in TTS:

GANs (generative adversarial networks)

A generator makes audio; a discriminator tries to tell it from real audio; each improves by competing. Sharp results and one-step generation, but training can be unstable. The workhorse of vocoders (HiFi-GAN).

VAEs (variational autoencoders)

Compress real data into a small random “code”, learn to decode it back, and keep the codes tidy so you can sample new ones. Used inside VITS.

Normalizing flows

A chain of exactly reversible transformations that bends a simple bell-curve into the shape of real data. Exact likelihoods; architecture constraints. Used by Glow-TTS and VITS.

Diffusion models

Learn to remove noise a little at a time. To generate, start from pure noise and denoise step by step. Excellent quality; many steps make them slow. Grad-TTS [paper] brought this to TTS.

Flow matching

A simpler, faster cousin of diffusion: learn a velocity field that carries noise to data along (nearly) straight paths. Good quality in a handful of steps. Introduced by Lipman et al. [paper] and used in TTS by Matcha-TTS [paper] and F5-TTS [paper].

Flow matching, as a story

Pour a bag of sand (random noise) on a table. Somewhere there's a sand-castle mould (the shape of real mel spectrograms). Pair each grain with a spot in the castle and draw a straight line between them. Training teaches a network one thing: “at this place and this moment, which way should a grain move, and how fast?” To generate, scatter fresh sand and follow the network's arrows for a few steps. The castle appears.

Try it: noise flowing to data

Each grey dot starts as random noise (t = 0) and travels in a straight line to a point of the target shape (t = 1): x_t = (1 − t)·x₀ + t·x₁. The network is trained to predict the velocity x₁ − x₀ from x_t and t alone. In a TTS model each “dot” is a whole mel spectrogram and the target shape is “a mel that says this sentence”.

Go deeper: the flow-matching loss and sampler 
# Training (x1 = real normalised mel, cond = text features stretched to frames)
x0 = torch.randn_like(x1)                      # noise
t  = torch.rand(batch)                          # random time in [0, 1]
xt = (1 - t) * x0 + t * x1                      # a point on the straight path
loss = ((v_net(xt, t, cond) - (x1 - x0)) ** 2).mean()

# Generation: Euler steps from noise to a mel
x = torch.randn(...)
for k in range(steps):                          # e.g. steps = 8
    x = x + v_net(x, k / steps, cond) / steps

Classifier-free guidance (CFG), a common add-on: during training sometimes drop the condition; at generation, push the prediction away from the unconditional one, v = v_uncond + w·(v_cond − v_uncond), for clearer, more on-text speech at the cost of an extra network call per step. See Ho & Salimans [paper].

Check yourself

Why does a plain L1-trained decoder sound muffled?

Averaging many valid deliveries gives a blurry, flat compromise: over-smoothing.

In flow matching, what does the network learn to predict?

It learns the direction and speed to move a noisy point toward real data at each time t.

12Vocoders: playing the sheet music

The mel spectrogram says which frequencies are loud when, roughly. It doesn't say the exact shape of every wiggle (the phase), and it blurs fine detail. The vocoder is a musician who must improvise all that missing detail, 24,000 times a second, so convincingly that you never notice.

  • Griffin–Lim (1984): no learning; iteratively guesses phase. Fast to set up, sounds metallic. Useful for debugging.
  • WaveNet (2016) [paper]: predicts one sample at a time with dilated convolutions. Beautiful and extremely slow.
  • WaveRNN, WaveGlow, Parallel WaveGAN, MelGAN: the race to make neural vocoders fast.
  • HiFi-GAN (2020) [paper]: a generator of transposed convolutions upsamples mel to waveform; multi-period and multi-scale discriminators judge it. High quality, real time on CPU. The long-time default.
  • BigVGAN (2022) [paper]: a large, universal HiFi-GAN-style vocoder that generalises to unseen voices.
  • Vocos (2023) [paper]: instead of upsampling in the time domain, predicts STFT magnitude and phase at frame rate and uses an inverse STFT to make audio. Far fewer operations, so very fast.
Practical advice: as a beginner, don't train a vocoder. Use a pretrained universal one (Vocos or HiFi-GAN/BigVGAN checkpoints) and match its mel settings exactly. Train your own only when you need a new sample rate or the last bit of quality.

Check yourself

What makes Vocos faster than time-domain GAN vocoders?

Working at frame rate and finishing with a cheap inverse STFT avoids heavy upsampling layers.

13The modern landscape

End-to-end: VITS (2021)

VITS [paper] joins acoustic model and vocoder into one network trained together: a VAE whose decoder is a HiFi-GAN-style generator, a normalizing flow, MAS for alignment, and a stochastic duration predictor. Text in, waveform out. Fast and natural; the backbone of many open voices, such as Piper's.

Style and expressiveness: StyleTTS 2 (2023)

StyleTTS 2 [paper] samples a “style vector” with diffusion and uses large speech-model discriminators. Very natural single-speaker quality; the compact Kokoro-82M model builds on its design.

Speech as tokens: codec language models (2023→)

A neural audio codec (e.g. EnCodec [paper]) compresses audio into a short sequence of discrete tokens. Then a GPT-style language model writes those tokens, just as a chatbot writes words. VALL-E [paper] showed this can copy a new voice from a 3-second prompt (zero-shot cloning). Powerful and expressive; large, slow, and can hallucinate (skip or repeat) like early Tacotron.

Flow-matching transformers: F5-TTS (2024)

F5-TTS [paper] drops explicit durations entirely: it pads the text to the target length and lets a large diffusion transformer, trained with flow matching, work out the alignment itself. Strong zero-shot cloning.

Small and on-device

A separate race aims for tiny, fast, offline models that run on phones, laptops and Raspberry Pis: Piper (VITS-based, ONNX) [repo], Kokoro-82M [model], KittenTTS [repo]. Here the hard design question isn't “best quality at any cost” but “best quality per megabyte and per millisecond”.

14Build a TTS model from scratch

Time to build. You'll make TinyTTS: a ~3.7M-parameter acoustic model in the FastSpeech / Glow-TTS family. It learns its own alignment with MAS, predicts durations, decodes mel spectrograms, and uses the pretrained Vocos vocoder for audio. Every file below was run end to end (prepare → train → speak) on a small synthetic test set before publishing this guide; you supply a real dataset.

TinyTTS at a glance

phoneme IDs→Embedding→3 × ConvBlock→4 × Transformer→h (one vector per phoneme)
h→μ projection → MAS (training)/Duration predictor (inference)→Length regulator→6 × ConvBlock decoder→mel→Vocos→audio

Three losses train it together: mel (L1 between predicted and real mel), prior (keeps μ meaningful so MAS finds good alignments), duration (MSE on log durations from MAS).

Step 3 · The model

Read it top to bottom: MAS, then the building blocks from chapter 7, then the model's forward (training) and synthesize (inference). Running the file performs a self-test.

"""A minimal, teachable TTS acoustic model (Glow-TTS / FastSpeech-style), built from scratch.

text ids -> encoder -> (MAS alignment during training | duration predictor at inference)
         -> length regulator -> decoder -> normalised log-mel -> (pretrained Vocos) -> audio
"""
import math
import numpy as np
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F


# ---------- Monotonic Alignment Search (Glow-TTS) ----------
def mas(log_p: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
    """log_p: [T_text, T_mel] log-likelihood of mel frame j under phoneme i.
    Returns a 0/1 path [T_text, T_mel]: every frame gets exactly one phoneme,
    phonemes are used in order, none is skipped. Needs T_mel >= T_text."""
    T, M = log_p.shape
    Q = np.full((T, M), -np.inf)
    Q[0, 0] = log_p[0, 0]
    for j in range(1, M):
        for i in range(min(j + 1, T)):
            stay = Q[i, j - 1]
            move = Q[i - 1, j - 1] if i > 0 else -np.inf
            Q[i, j] = max(stay, move) + log_p[i, j]
    path = np.zeros((T, M), dtype=np.float32)
    i = T - 1
    for j in range(M - 1, -1, -1):
        path[i, j] = 1
        if j > 0 and i > 0 and (i == j or Q[i - 1, j - 1] >= Q[i, j - 1]):
            i -= 1
    return path


def mask_from_lengths(lengths, max_len=None):
    max_len = max_len or int(lengths.max())
    return torch.arange(max_len, device=lengths.device)[None] < lengths[:, None]  # [B, T] True = real


# ---------- Building blocks ----------
class ConvBlock(nn.Module):
    """Conv1d -> GELU -> LayerNorm -> dropout, with a residual connection."""
    def __init__(self, d, k=5, p=0.1):
        super().__init__()
        self.conv = nn.Conv1d(d, d, k, padding=k // 2)
        self.norm = nn.LayerNorm(d)
        self.drop = nn.Dropout(p)

    def forward(self, x, mask):  # x [B, T, d], mask [B, T]
        y = self.conv((x * mask[..., None]).transpose(1, 2)).transpose(1, 2)
        return x + self.drop(self.norm(F.gelu(y)))


class Encoder(nn.Module):
    """Phoneme ids -> context-aware vectors: embedding, local convs, then self-attention."""
    def __init__(self, n_symbols, d=192, n_conv=3, n_attn=4, heads=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.emb = nn.Embedding(n_symbols, d)
        self.convs = nn.ModuleList([ConvBlock(d) for _ in range(n_conv)])
        layer = nn.TransformerEncoderLayer(d, heads, 4 * d, dropout=0.1, batch_first=True, norm_first=True)
        self.attn = nn.TransformerEncoder(layer, n_attn, enable_nested_tensor=False)

    def forward(self, ids, mask):
        x = self.emb(ids) * math.sqrt(self.emb.embedding_dim)
        for c in self.convs:
            x = c(x, mask)
        return self.attn(x, src_key_padding_mask=~mask) * mask[..., None]


class DurationPredictor(nn.Module):
    """Predicts log(1 + frames) for every phoneme."""
    def __init__(self, d=192):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.ModuleList([ConvBlock(d, k=3, p=0.3) for _ in range(2)])
        self.out = nn.Linear(d, 1)

    def forward(self, h, mask):
        for c in self.net:
            h = c(h, mask)
        return self.out(h).squeeze(-1) * mask


class Decoder(nn.Module):
    """Frame-rate vectors -> mel frames."""
    def __init__(self, d=192, n_mels=100, n_layers=6):
        super().__init__()
        self.blocks = nn.ModuleList([ConvBlock(d) for _ in range(n_layers)])
        self.out = nn.Linear(d, n_mels)

    def forward(self, h, mask):
        for b in self.blocks:
            h = b(h, mask)
        return self.out(h) * mask[..., None]


# ---------- The model ----------
class TinyTTS(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, n_symbols, d=192, n_mels=100):
        super().__init__()
        self.encoder = Encoder(n_symbols, d)
        self.to_mu = nn.Linear(d, n_mels)        # each phoneme's "average" mel frame, used for alignment
        self.duration = DurationPredictor(d)
        self.decoder = Decoder(d, n_mels)

    def forward(self, ids, id_lens, mel, mel_lens):
        """Training. ids [B, T], mel [B, M, n_mels] (normalised log-mel). Returns a dict of losses."""
        x_mask, y_mask = mask_from_lengths(id_lens), mask_from_lengths(mel_lens)
        h = self.encoder(ids, x_mask)
        mu = self.to_mu(h)                                              # [B, T, n_mels]

        with torch.no_grad():                                           # 1) find the alignment
            log_p = -0.5 * torch.cdist(mu.float(), mel.float()) ** 2    # [B, T, M]
            attn = torch.zeros_like(log_p)
            for b in range(ids.size(0)):
                t, m = int(id_lens[b]), int(mel_lens[b])
                attn[b, :t, :m] = torch.from_numpy(mas(log_p[b, :t, :m].cpu().numpy()))
        durations = attn.sum(-1)                                        # frames per phoneme

        h_up = attn.transpose(1, 2) @ h                                 # 2) length regulator: [B, M, d]
        mel_hat = self.decoder(h_up, y_mask)                            # 3) decode
        mu_up = attn.transpose(1, 2) @ mu

        n = y_mask.sum() * mel.size(-1)
        loss_mel = (mel_hat - mel).abs().sum() / n                      # L1 reconstruction
        loss_prior = ((mu_up - mel) ** 2 * y_mask[..., None]).sum() / n  # keeps mu meaningful for MAS
        logd = self.duration(h.detach(), x_mask)
        loss_dur = ((logd - torch.log1p(durations)) ** 2 * x_mask).sum() / x_mask.sum()
        return {"mel": loss_mel, "prior": loss_prior, "dur": loss_dur}

    @torch.no_grad()
    def synthesize(self, ids, speed=1.0):
        """Inference for one utterance. ids [1, T]. Returns normalised log-mel [1, M, n_mels]."""
        mask = torch.ones_like(ids, dtype=torch.bool)
        h = self.encoder(ids, mask)
        frames = torch.clamp(torch.round(torch.expm1(self.duration(h, mask)) / speed), min=1).long()[0]
        h_up = torch.repeat_interleave(h[0], frames, dim=0)[None]      # length regulator
        return self.decoder(h_up, torch.ones(h_up.shape[:2], dtype=torch.bool))


if __name__ == "__main__":
    # Smoke test on random tensors: shapes, a backward pass, and inference.
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    model = TinyTTS(n_symbols=100)
    print(f"{sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters()) / 1e6:.2f}M parameters")
    ids = torch.randint(1, 100, (2, 12)); id_lens = torch.tensor([12, 9])
    mel = torch.randn(2, 60, 100); mel_lens = torch.tensor([60, 41])
    opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=5e-4)
    for step in range(3):
        losses = model(ids, id_lens, mel, mel_lens)
        loss = sum(losses.values())
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward()
        torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0); opt.step()
        print(step, {k: round(v.item(), 3) for k, v in losses.items()})
    model.eval()
    print("inference mel:", tuple(model.synthesize(ids[:1]).shape))
    # MAS sanity check: a clear diagonal must be recovered.
    true = np.repeat(np.arange(4), [3, 5, 2, 6])
    lp = np.where(np.arange(4)[:, None] == true[None], 0.0, -5.0)
    print("MAS durations:", mas(lp).sum(1).astype(int).tolist(), "expected [3, 5, 2, 6]")

Step 4 · Train

Groups utterances of similar length into batches, warms up the learning rate, clips gradients, logs losses, and saves a checkpoint every 5,000 steps. Run it on a GPU; start listening around 20k–50k steps.

"""Step 2: train TinyTTS. Usage: python train.py data/ runs/first [steps]"""
import json, random, sys
from pathlib import Path
import torch
from tiny_tts import TinyTTS

data, run = Path(sys.argv[1]), Path(sys.argv[2])
steps = int(sys.argv[3]) if len(sys.argv) > 3 else 100_000
run.mkdir(parents=True, exist_ok=True)
meta, index = json.load(open(data / "meta.json")), json.load(open(data / "index.json"))
mean, std = torch.tensor(meta["mean"]), torch.tensor(meta["std"])
random.seed(0); random.shuffle(index)
val, train = index[:50], index[50:]
device = "cuda" if torch.cuda.is_available() else "cpu"


def batch(items):
    """Pad a list of utterances into tensors (0 = padding)."""
    mels = [(torch.load(data / "mels" / f"{u['id']}.pt") - mean) / std for u in items]
    ids = [torch.tensor(u["ids"]) for u in items]
    pad = torch.nn.utils.rnn.pad_sequence
    return (pad(ids, batch_first=True), torch.tensor([len(i) for i in ids]),
            pad(mels, batch_first=True), torch.tensor([len(m) for m in mels]))


def batches(items, max_frames=6000):
    """Group utterances of similar length so little compute is wasted on padding."""
    items = sorted(items, key=lambda u: u["frames"])
    groups, cur = [], []
    for u in items:
        if cur and (len(cur) + 1) * u["frames"] > max_frames:
            groups.append(cur); cur = []
        cur.append(u)
    groups.append(cur)
    random.shuffle(groups)
    return groups


model = TinyTTS(len(meta["symbols"])).to(device)
opt = torch.optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=5e-4, betas=(0.9, 0.98), weight_decay=0.01)
sched = torch.optim.lr_scheduler.LambdaLR(opt, lambda s: min(1.0, (s + 1) / 2000))  # warm-up
step = 0
while step < steps:
    for group in batches(train):
        ids, il, mel, ml = (t.to(device) for t in batch(group))
        losses = model(ids, il, mel, ml)
        loss = losses["mel"] + losses["prior"] + losses["dur"]
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward()
        torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
        opt.step(); sched.step(); step += 1
        if step % 100 == 0:
            print(step, {k: round(v.item(), 4) for k, v in losses.items()}, flush=True)
        if step % 5000 == 0 or step == steps:
            model.eval()
            with torch.no_grad():
                v = batch(val[:16]); v_loss = model(*(t.to(device) for t in v))["mel"].item()
            model.train()
            print(f"step {step} val mel L1 {v_loss:.4f}", flush=True)
            torch.save({"model": model.state_dict(), "meta": meta, "step": step}, run / "ckpt.pt")
        if step >= steps:
            break

Step 6 · Upgrade the decoder with flow matching

Once TinyTTS talks, you'll hear the over-smoothing from chapter 11. Replace the L1 decoder with this flow-matching decoder: condition it on the length-regulated encoder output, train with flow_matching_loss instead of the L1 mel loss, and generate with sample. Expect crisper, livelier audio for a few extra network calls per sentence.

"""Upgrade: replace the L1 decoder with a flow-matching decoder (as in Matcha-TTS / Lipman et al.)."""
import math
import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.nn.functional as F


class TimeEmbedding(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d):
        super().__init__()
        self.d = d
        self.mlp = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d, d), nn.GELU(), nn.Linear(d, d))

    def forward(self, t):  # t [B] in [0, 1]
        half = self.d // 2
        freqs = torch.exp(-math.log(10000) * torch.arange(half, device=t.device) / half)
        ang = 1000 * t[:, None] * freqs[None]
        return self.mlp(torch.cat([ang.sin(), ang.cos()], -1))


class VectorField(nn.Module):
    """v(x_t, t, condition): predicts the direction from noise to the real mel."""
    def __init__(self, d=192, n_mels=100, n_layers=6):
        super().__init__()
        self.inp = nn.Linear(n_mels + d, d)
        self.time = TimeEmbedding(d)
        self.convs = nn.ModuleList([nn.Conv1d(d, d, 5, padding=2) for _ in range(n_layers)])
        self.norms = nn.ModuleList([nn.LayerNorm(d) for _ in range(n_layers)])
        self.out = nn.Linear(d, n_mels)

    def forward(self, x_t, t, cond, mask):  # x_t [B, M, n_mels], cond [B, M, d], mask [B, M]
        h = self.inp(torch.cat([x_t, cond], -1)) + self.time(t)[:, None]
        for conv, norm in zip(self.convs, self.norms):
            y = conv((h * mask[..., None]).transpose(1, 2)).transpose(1, 2)
            h = h + F.gelu(norm(y))
        return self.out(h) * mask[..., None]


def flow_matching_loss(vf, mel, cond, mask):
    """Straight paths from noise x0 to data x1: x_t = (1 - t) x0 + t x1, target velocity x1 - x0."""
    x1 = mel
    x0 = torch.randn_like(x1)
    t = torch.rand(x1.size(0), device=x1.device)
    x_t = (1 - t)[:, None, None] * x0 + t[:, None, None] * x1
    v = vf(x_t, t, cond, mask)
    err = (v - (x1 - x0)) ** 2 * mask[..., None]
    return err.sum() / (mask.sum() * x1.size(-1))


@torch.no_grad()
def sample(vf, cond, mask, n_mels=100, steps=8):
    """Euler integration from noise (t = 0) to a mel (t = 1)."""
    x = torch.randn(cond.size(0), cond.size(1), n_mels, device=cond.device)
    for k in range(steps):
        t = torch.full((cond.size(0),), k / steps, device=cond.device)
        x = x + vf(x, t, cond, mask) / steps
    return x


if __name__ == "__main__":
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    vf = VectorField()
    cond = torch.randn(2, 50, 192); mel = torch.randn(2, 50, 100); mask = torch.ones(2, 50, dtype=torch.bool)
    loss = flow_matching_loss(vf, mel, cond, mask); loss.backward()
    print("fm loss", round(loss.item(), 3), "sample", tuple(sample(vf, cond, mask).shape))

When things go wrong

  • Pure noise or garble: mel settings don't match the vocoder, or you forgot to un-normalise before Vocos.
  • Mumbled words: alignment hasn't formed yet; train longer, check the prior loss is falling, check phonemes are correct.
  • Robotic, flat voice: over-smoothing; that's what step 6 is for.
  • Wrong speed or swallowed sounds: duration predictor; try more training or the speed argument.
  • Loss becomes NaN: lower the learning rate, keep gradient clipping on, check for empty or silent clips.

15Measuring a voice, and making it small

Quality

  • MOS (Mean Opinion Score): listeners rate naturalness 1–5; the gold standard, but slow and costly. CMOS compares two systems side by side.
  • UTMOS [paper]: a network trained to predict MOS. Fast and great for comparing your own experiments; not a replacement for real listeners.
  • WER (word error rate): transcribe your audio with a speech recogniser like Whisper [paper] and compare with the input text. Catches mumbling, skipping and mispronunciation.
  • Speaker similarity: for cloning, cosine similarity between speaker embeddings of the reference and the output.

Always compare against real recordings scored the same way: that's the ceiling.

Speed and size

  • RTF (real-time factor): compute time ÷ audio duration. RTF 0.1 means 10× faster than real time.
  • Latency to first audio: what users feel. Streaming (synthesising sentence by sentence or chunk by chunk) cuts it dramatically.
  • Parameters and file size: 1M parameters ≈ 4 MB in fp32, 2 MB in fp16, 1 MB in int8.
  • Quantisation: storing weights in 16 or 8 bits instead of 32. Smaller and often faster; always re-check quality afterwards.
  • ONNX + ONNX Runtime: export the trained network to a portable graph that runs fast on CPUs without PyTorch, on almost any platform.

16Designing a new architecture

Every model in this guide began as someone noticing a specific pain (“attention skips words”, “diffusion is too slow”, “GPUs are too expensive for my users”) and proposing one idea to fix it. Inventing an architecture isn't magic; it's a disciplined loop of observe → hypothesise → change one thing → measure fairly → keep or discard.

1 · Pick the axis you want to win on

Quality, speed, size, robustness, expressiveness, data efficiency, controllability, multilinguality. You rarely win all. Write the target as numbers: “UTMOS within 0.1 of baseline at half the parameters”.

2 · Know the design space

  • Input: characters, phonemes, or both; prosody or emotion tags.
  • Alignment: attention, external aligner, MAS, or implicit (padded text, as in F5-TTS).
  • Encoder: convolutions, transformers, or a pretrained language model.
  • Decoder objective: regression, GAN, VAE, flow, diffusion, flow matching, codec tokens.
  • Decoder network: convolutional, U-Net, transformer, hybrid; at what time resolution.
  • Output: mel + vocoder, STFT, codec tokens, or waveform (end-to-end).
  • Inference budget: number of steps, guidance, streaming.

3 · Start from a reproduced baseline

Train a known model (TinyTTS from chapter 14, or a public one) on your data and measure it. Without a baseline you can't tell whether your idea helped.

4 · Run ablations

  • Change one thing at a time; keep data, steps and compute identical.
  • Use the same evaluation set and metrics (UTMOS, WER, RTF, parameters) every time.
  • Train more than one seed for promising changes; small differences are often noise. Report confidence intervals (e.g. bootstrap over utterances).
  • Listen to the same sentences across variants. Metrics miss things ears catch.
  • Screen many ideas with short runs, then give the survivors full training.

5 · Write it down

One-page design doc template

  1. Problem: what's wrong with current models, in one sentence, with evidence.
  2. Idea: the one change, and why it should help.
  3. Prediction: which metric moves, by how much.
  4. Baseline and ablations: exactly what you will train and compare.
  5. Budget: GPU hours, data, deadline.
  6. Kill criterion: the result that would make you drop the idea.

6 · Read papers like a researcher

First pass: abstract, figures, conclusion. Second pass: the architecture diagram and the ablation table (what did each part contribute?). Third pass: the method details and the code. Then try to redraw the architecture from memory using the toolbox from chapter 7.
